## 1 Introduction
From its earliest days, artificial intelligence (AI) grew out of a scientific desire to explain observed phenomena. Classical statistical models, such as linear regression, reveal the contribution of each covariate through their coefficients, while rule-based expert systems like MYCIN could enumerate the exact production rules behind every recommendation [1]. Decision-tree induction extended this tradition by yielding hierarchical if-then structures whose internal tests were immediately intelligible to domain experts [2]. Under this first wave of AI, interpretability was an intrinsic property rather than an optional add-on. The landscape changed dramatically with the advent of large, data-hungry neural networks. Deep learning systems now achieve very high accuracy in vision, language, and strategic decision-making, but do so by encoding complex computations in millions or billions of inscrutable parameters [3]. When such models are deployed in medicine, finance, or criminal justice, stakeholders justifiably ask: Why did the system produce this output, and can we trust it? Failure to answer these questions undermines accountability, fairness, and safety [4]. Hence, a second wave of research, explainable AI (XAI), has sought to bridge the gap between predictive performance and human understanding [5,6]. Most mainstream XAI techniques probe models from the outside. Model-agnostic frameworks such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) approximate local decision boundaries using interpretable surrogate models [7,8], while SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) attributes importance scores to input features based on Shapley values [9,10]. Although invaluable for model auditing and debugging, these post hoc explanations are primarily behavioral approximations; they describe statistical associations between inputs and outputs rather than revealing the internal logic by which a model produces its outputs. Mechanistic interpretability offers a complementary, inside-out perspective by analyzing the internal algorithms that neural networks learn. The following box presents a concise definition. Definition of Mechanistic Interpretability Mechanistic interpretability is a research paradigm that seeks to reverse-engineer neural networks by identifying internal components, such as neurons, circuits, and activation pathways that either contribute to or causally influence specific behaviors. Unlike post hoc methods that rely on inputoutput correlations, mechanistic interpretability emphasizes structural and algorithmic mechanisms encoded in a model's parameters and activations. It encompasses both causal analyses (e.g., interventions and ablations) and descriptive methods (e.g., feature visualization, sparse autoencoders) that reveal representational organization without direct causal testing. It draws inspiration from program analysis and neuroscience, treating models as executable systems whose logic can be decoded and mapped onto human-legible subroutines [11,12]. ## 2.1 Interpretability in ML: An Overview
Interpretability in machine learning refers to how well humans can understand a model's decisionmaking process, a critical aspect in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, finance, and law where transparency complements predictive accuracy [18]. Early interpretable systems included MYCIN (1975), which used symbolic if-then rules [1], and decision trees like ID3 (1986) with humaninspectable hierarchies [2]. Later, algorithms such as TREPAN (1990s) extracted symbolic rules from neural networks [19]. The deep learning era introduced new interpretability tools for non-linear models: gradient-based saliency maps [20], DeconvNets [21], and model-agnostic approaches like LIME [7] and SHAP [9]. Techniques such as Integrated Gradients [22] and DeepLIFT [23] further improved gradient-based explanations with axiomatic guarantees. Together, these methods-from rule-based reasoning to post hoc visualization and attribution-trace the evolution of interpretability toward mechanistic and causal understanding in black-box systems (Figure 1).

## 2.2 The Shift Toward Mechanistic Interpretability
Interpretability is vital for fairness, transparency, and trust in machine learning, particularly in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and finance. It enables bias detection, feature attribution, model debugging, and ethical alignment by making the model's decision processes more understandable [18]. Interpretability techniques are often categorized into intrinsic methods (e.g., decision trees, generalized linear models) and post hoc methods (e.g., LIME, SHAP), and further divided into global (model-wide) and local (instance-level) explanations. Between post hoc interpretability and fully mechanistic analysis lies an important intermediary stage: Explainable-by-Design models. These approaches embed interpretability directly within the architecture, ensuring that the reasoning process remains human-readable without external explanation layers. Classical examples include GAMs and rule-based systems, which balance predictive power with transparency [24][25][26]. More recent neural extensions, such as Self-Explaining Neural Networks (SENNs) [27], prototype-based architectures (ProtoPNet) [28], and Concept Bottleneck Models [29], internalize explanation mechanisms so that latent features correspond to explicit human concepts. This family of models represents an evolutionary bridge toward mechanistic interpretability [30,31], where the focus shifts from explaining outputs to designing networks whose internal logic is intrinsically interpretable. A parallel progression has emerged under the Hybrid and Automated Discovery paradigm, which combines architectural transparency with scalable analysis tools. Methods such as Automated Circuit Discovery (ACDC) [32] and Sparse Autoencoder (SAE)-based feature extraction [33] automate the identification of causal subcircuits and disentangled representations, reducing the manual effort traditionally required in mechanistic interpretability. Mechanistic interpretability moves beyond post hoc analysis by reverse-engineering the internal computations of neural networks. Instead of correlating inputs and outputs, it identifies functional subgraphs-neurons, attention heads, and residual streams-that causally implement behaviors. Early work on feature visualization [34] evolved into composable tools combining saliency maps and dimensionality reduction [35], culminating in the "circuits microscope" for tracing activations [11]. Subsequent advances included mathematical decomposition of transformer circuits [36], discovery of induction heads for in-context learning [37], and causal scrubbing to test mechanistic hypotheses via ablation [38]. Recent methods extend this toolkit with path patching for activation control [39], causal abstraction for mapping algorithmic variables [40], and activation lens approaches for layer-wise prediction analysis [41,42]. ## 3 Theoretical Foundations


## 3.1 What is Mechanistic Interpretability?
Mechanistic interpretability, summarized earlier in Knowledge Box 1, extends beyond post hoc correlation analysis by directly examining how internal components such as neurons, attention heads, and circuits implement specific computations. It focuses on mapping these components to human-understandable functions through causal or structural analysis of a model's parameters and activations. While mechanistic interpretability directly examines model internals, it is generally regarded as a specialized branch of post hoc explanation methods-focused on reverse-engineering trained networks rather than providing transparency by design. This taxonomy aligns with Figure 2, where mechanistic interpretability is positioned as a white-box, causal subcategory within the broader post hoc family. Pioneered by Olah and collaborators through the Transformer Circuits thread [11,36], this field introduced a vocabulary for decomposing networks into modular circuits akin to subroutines in classical software. These circuits often implement reusable computational patterns such as induction, equality-checking, or syntactic alignment. As shown in Figure 3, mechanistic interpretability follows a reverse-engineering paradigm, emphasizing structural and causal dissection rather than abstract concept association [44]. The analogy between neural networks and compiled programs underpins mechanistic interpretability. In this view, mechanistic analysis resembles reverse engineering, where researchers decode a network's virtual machine to understand how internal representations give rise to observed behavior. This framing highlights both the task's complexity and its feasibility for achieving mechanistic understanding in large models. Mechanistic interpretability has expanded from toy examples to studies of large language models and vision transformers, progressing through analyses of smaller subnetworks, reusable mechanisms, and measurable benchmarks [46]. By emphasizing internal validity, algorithmic transparency, and causal alignment, mechanistic interpretability advances safer and more auditable AI. As it matures, it is increasingly viewed as a core requirement for governing intelligent systems [47]. In addition to the post hoc, explainable-by-design, and mechanistic paradigms discussed earlier, two emerging directions are worth noting. Self-Explaining Architectures incorporate interpretability within the model's structure itself, allowing latent representations or prototypes to directly correspond to human concepts. Representative examples include SENN [27], ProtoPNet [28], and Concept Bottleneck Models [29]. The Hybrid and Automated Discovery category reflects recent progress in scaling interpretability through automation. Frameworks such as ACDC [32] and SAEs [33] search large networks to uncover causal subcircuits and disentangled features with minimal manual effort. These approaches bridge manual circuit tracing and fully mechanistic analysis, combining architectural transparency with computational scalability. ## 3.2 Features in Mechanistic Interpretability
In mechanistic interpretability, a feature represents the smallest semantic unit of meaning in a neural network. It is typically viewed as a direction in activation space corresponding to a latent input property-such as a curve in an image or the final token in a phrase like "Eiffel Tower"-that emerges during training and can exist in linear superposition across neurons or dimensions [14]. Unlike traditional interpretability, which focuses on global input-output correlations, mechanistic interpretability treats features as internal causal mechanisms, revealing how knowledge is encoded and applied within the model. This reframes a feature from an input artifact to a computational abstraction embedded in the model's circuits and weights [15].

Suppose a feature direction v corresponds to the concept of lane markings. The scalar activation 𝑎 = v ⊤ h quantifies how strongly lane features are present in a given region of the image. Importantly, multiple features may be linearly superimposed across neurons due to the superposition hypothesis, which asserts that neural representations compress many semantically meaningful features into shared dimensions to optimize capacity [11]. This mechanism underlies a key challenge: a single neuron does not necessarily represent a single interpretable concept. Instead, interpretability often improves when activations are analyzed in a rotated basis typically obtained via sparse dictionary learning or non-negative matrix factorization thus transforming neuron space into feature space [44].

## 3.2.3 Feature-Function Alignment.
To link latent features with interpretable mechanisms, researchers assess their causal impact on downstream computations-tracking how features propagate through layers, influence circuits, and shape final outputs such as logits or decisions. As shown by Kulkarni [59], isolating features responsible for tasks like factual recall or syntactic parsing involves identifying attention heads or pathways where such information is both represented and functionally active. In convolutional networks, early-layer features capture simple visual motifs that are later combined into abstract representations, reflecting the hierarchical nature of feature composition. Example. Consider a language model trained to predict the next word in a sentence. Suppose a particular feature encodes plurality. For two sentences, The cat runs and The cats run, a feature_swap transfers the activation corresponding to the plurality feature from the plural sentence to the singular one. If this intervention changes the model's prediction from runs to run, it indicates that the swapped feature causally governs plural behavior. Averaging such outcomes across multiple examples yields the IIA score, quantifying how reliably this feature influences linguistic agreement. High IIA indicates that the manipulated feature is causally aligned with the model's decision-making process [45].

## 3.3 Circuits in Mechanistic Interpretability
In the mechanistic interpretability paradigm, the notion of a circuit is central to linking low-level computations with high-level algorithmic behavior. A circuit is a structured subgraph of neurons, layers, and weights that together perform a specific function-ranging from logical gating to factual retrieval or syntactic processing. Circuits serve as the fundamental computational units of neural networks, analogous to functional blocks in digital electronics or pathways in biological systems [11].  In the left column, curve detectors in similar orientations exhibit strong positive weights (red), resulting in excitatory activation. In the right column, detectors in opposing orientations show inhibitory patterns (blue weights), suppressing misaligned input. This illustrates how neural networks construct functional subgraphs with interpretable weight structures, enabling selective feature composition [11]. The concept gained prominence through the Circuits thread by Olah et al., which introduced methods to trace interpretable algorithms within trained models. By visualizing neuron activations that reliably correspond to patterns such as curves, textures, or semantic structures, researchers identified reusable computational motifs. For example, early vision circuits in convolutional models detect edges, corners, and composite objects through sequential layer activations [11]. Features form the atomic building blocks from which circuits are composed. Each circuit can be viewed as a structured arrangement of features that jointly implement a higher-level function-such as syntactic dependency tracking, token copying, or factual recall [11,36]. While an individual feature captures a specific activation pattern or direction in representation space, circuits emerge when multiple such features interact through attention heads or layer-to-layer transformations. In this sense, features provide the representational substrate, and circuits provide the computational organization that connects these representations to model behavior. This hierarchical relationship is reflected in Figure 1, where features underpin the formation of circuits along the progression from representational to algorithmic explanations.

These circuits are not hard-coded by design but emerge through training. Their functional specificity becomes evident when tracing how inputs propagate through them and contribute causally to outputs. ## 3.3.2 Induction Head Circuit: An Illustrative Example.
An induction head circuit is a canonical example of mechanistic structure in transformers. It operates by using query-key similarity to match repeated tokens in a context and uses the resulting attention to copy the following token. This circuit emerges when attention heads in later layers learn to attend to earlier positions with similar token content. The mechanism relies on both the query-key (QK) and value-output (VO) components working in unison to implement this algorithm. ## 3.3.3 Circuit Universality and Motifs.
Researchers have observed recurring circuits across diverse models and tasks, a phenomenon known as circuit universality. These include motifs for equality checking, positional encoding, syntactic dependency, and numerical reasoning [15]. Such recurrence suggests circuits act as reusable algorithmic components that generalize across architectures and training regimes. Figure 6 illustrates this by comparing models trained on similar tasks with disentangled counterparts. Although activation patterns differ, their feature-level circuits reveal common substructures-compact computational motifs that converge toward shared algorithmic patterns despite variations in initialization or architecture. This convergence supports the universality hypothesis in mechanistic interpretability. A taxonomy of motifs further classifies circuits into linear projection, logical comparison, and memory types, mirroring structural patterns found in programming and neuroscience [36].

## 3.3.4 Challenges in Circuit Discovery.
Despite progress, automating circuit discovery faces key challenges. First, the non-uniqueness of decompositions complicates identifiability, as multiple overlapping circuits can produce the same behavior [45]. Second, many circuits operate in high-dimensional superpositions, making them difficult to isolate with standard probing. Sparse dictionary learning and causal interventions offer partial solutions but require careful validation [44]. Finally, circuit validation remains open-hypothesized circuits must demonstrate predictive consistency, causal sufficiency, and robustness to ablation. Benchmarking frameworks and toy model "organisms" have been proposed to address this [11,44].

## 3.4 Core Theoretical Tools in Mechanistic Interpretability
Mechanistic interpretability has developed a set of core theoretical tools that offer a rigorous foundation for analyzing the inner workings of neural networks. These tools help researchers explain how representations are stored, how networks undergo structural shifts during training, and how specific functions are implemented through identifiable mechanisms. This subsection introduces three such tools: superposition and polysemanticity, grokking and phase transitions, and induction heads and indirect object identification.

## 3.4.1 Superposition and Polysemanticity.
Superposition refers to the phenomenon where multiple distinct features are represented along overlapping directions in the activation space of a neural network. Rather than allocating a unique neuron or dimension to each interpretable feature, networks, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), tend to learn distributed representations in which individual neurons participate in encoding multiple, potentially unrelated features [14]. This leads to polysemanticity, where a single neuron or direction may be active for various input conditions depending on context. This behavior is not merely an artifact but a space-efficient strategy induced by optimization under parameter constraints. While this improves model capacity, it complicates interpretability, as the activation of one neuron does not uniquely correspond to a single human-interpretable concept [11,44].

## 3.4.2 Grokking and Phase Transitions.
Grokking refers to the counterintuitive behavior where a neural network, after appearing to overfit the training set with poor generalization, suddenly undergoes a sharp transition and begins to generalize effectively after many additional gradient steps. This phenomenon is typically observed in settings with small synthetic datasets, such as modular arithmetic, and is closely associated with internal reorganization of model representations [14]. Grokking can be understood as a phase transition, where the network's internal structure moves from a memorization-based regime to an algorithmic regime. The transition is often preceded by increasing circuit-like organization, as evidenced by shifts in neuron activations and layer roles. These observations have inspired mechanistic studies of how inductive biases and loss gradients steer networks toward interpretable solutions over time [45].

## 3.4.3 Induction Heads and Indirect Object Identification.
Induction heads-attention patterns that implement token-copying behavior in transformers (as described previously)-serve as canonical examples of learned mechanistic circuits [59]. They play a central role in auto-regressive models by supporting next-token prediction and sequence continuation. Building on this foundation, related circuit motifs underpin higher-level linguistic phenomena such as IOI, where attention heads specialize in resolving coreference and syntactic roles [15]. Such circuits are often localized and experimentally validated through interventions, making them recurring subjects of mechanistic interpretability research. Together, these mechanisms-superposition, phase transitions, and circuit motifs-form a foundational toolkit for reverse-engineering the representations and computations learned by modern neural networks.

## 4 Methodologies


## 4.1 Manual Circuit Tracing
Manual circuit tracing involves manually identifying and labeling the subnets (circuits) within a model that are responsible for a given behavior. In this approach, researchers interpret a neural network as a directed computational graph of neurons and attention heads, then isolate a subgraph that implements the target function [13]. They examine activation patterns (e.g., which attention heads attend to which tokens) and follow the flow of information between layers to hypothesize how the model computes a certain output. By inspecting attention matrices and weight patterns, researchers hand-label important components-for example, an attention head might be labeled as a copying or name-mover head if it transfers entity tokens across positions. Such attention-flow tracing can reveal distinct roles for different heads or neurons. Olah et al. [11] pioneered this by visualizing how image classifiers detect curves and textures; in transformers, this translates to tracking chains of attention that carry information from input to output tokens. Manual Circuit Tracing: Key Steps 1. Select a behavior: Identify a specific model behavior (e.g., name copying). 3. Form hypotheses: Propose which heads or layers implement the function. 4. Trace components: Manually follow attention and activation paths to isolate circuits. A prominent case study is the IOI circuit in GPT-2 Small. In this task (identifying which person a pronoun refers to in a sentence), researchers manually uncovered a set of attention heads and MLP neurons that collaborate to solve the problem [13,44]. By hand-tracing attention patterns, they found that certain heads attend to the earlier occurrence of a name and then write that name into the residual stream at a later position, effectively carrying out the core IOI logic [46]. Researchers validated these interpretations by ablating those heads to confirm their causal role [61]. This manual mapping of the IOI circuit provided a human-understandable algorithm: GPT-2's solution could be summarized as find all names in the prompt, filter out the one that appears twice, and output the remaining name. Manual circuit tracing, while labor-intensive, demonstrates that even large networks contain decipherable computational subgraphs, and insights from such case studies have guided later automated methods [32].

## 4.2 Intervention-Based Techniques
Intervention-based approaches form a foundational pillar of mechanistic interpretability by altering a model's internal activations or structural components and observing the resulting causal effects on behavior. Unlike attribution-based methods such as saliency or Shapley values-which quantify the marginal contribution of inputs but generally operate at the correlation level-intervention-based techniques directly manipulate hidden representations to test causal hypotheses about internal computation [11]. While Shapley values can, under certain assumptions, approximate causal influence in input space, they do not intervene on internal mechanisms and therefore serve a complementary rather than competing role. Concretely, they assess whether a specific attention head, MLP neuron, or subcircuit is necessary or sufficient for a target behavior [39,46]. Two canonical interventions are: (i) activation patching, which transfers activations from a clean run to a corrupted one to test sufficiency and routing; and (ii) component ablation, which silences or replaces an element to test necessity. When such manipulations yield consistent, measurable changes in predictions (e.g., logit-difference shifts), the affected component is causally implicated in the computation [40]. Care must be taken to mitigate distribution shifts, especially for ablations that substitute baselines, to ensure observed changes reflect genuine causal involvement. These tools have uncovered core functional circuits in large language models-such as induction heads for in-context learning and multi-stage attention patterns in IOI-and are now being extended to vision-language architectures, demonstrating broad versatility [32,37,44]. While Sections 4.2.1-4.2.2 introduce causal interventions, Section 4.3.1 presents complementary observational methods that attribute information flow without modification.

## 4.2.1 Activation Patching.
Activation patching (also called causal tracing or causal mediation) evaluates whether specific intermediate activations are sufficient carriers of task-relevant information. A model is run on both a clean input that elicits correct behavior and a corrupted input that disrupts it. Activations from the clean run are then substituted into the corrupted one at a particular layer or attention head while holding all other states fixed [39,46]. If this restores the expected output (e.g., improves the logit difference toward the correct token), the patched site lies on a causal route for the behavior. Conversely, injecting corrupted activations into a clean run can reveal whether disrupting that signal causes failure, though strict necessity is better evaluated through ablation (Section 4.2.2). Patching can be applied at multiple granularities-from single neurons to full attention heads or multi-hop paths-to trace how information propagates across the network. It has been key to isolating mechanistic subcircuits such as the IOI circuit in GPT-2 and to identifying cross-modal integration layers in vision-language transformers [62,63]. In practice, its effects are quantified through output metrics such as Δ-logit difference or accuracy gain, with paired clean-corrupt inputs ensuring consistent causal inference and minimizing distributional drift.

## 4.2.2 Head/Neuron Ablation.
Ablation selectively disables a neuron, attention head, or small module-typically by zeroing its output or replacing it with a neutral baseline (e.g., dataset mean)to measure its necessity for a behavior [61,64]. If task performance degrades, the ablated element is considered causally required. Unlike activation patching (Section 4.2.1), which inserts signals to test sufficiency, ablation removes capacity to test necessity. Ablation studies in transformers have revealed head-and neuron-level roles in syntax tracking, token copying, and positional reasoning [44]. For instance, disabling IOI-related attention heads in GPT-2 disrupts pronoun resolution, empirically confirming their contribution [62]. However, because networks exhibit redundancy and superposition, performance drops may understate functional dependence; weak effects often signal feature overlap rather than irrelevance. Typical evaluation metrics include accuracy decline or logit-difference change, and baselines are carefully chosen to avoid out-ofdistribution artifacts. Together, patching and ablation establish a rigorous experimental foundation for causal verification in neural circuits.

## 4.2.3 Path Patching.
Path patching extends intervention-based interpretability by modifying sequences of components-rather than isolated units-within a model's computation graph. It replaces a series of internal activations from a clean run into corresponding locations in a corrupted run, enabling analysis of information flow and causal dependencies. A one-hop patch swaps the output of a single attention head, while multi-hop patching propagates such replacements across multiple layers. This approach reveals minimal sub-circuits sufficient to transfer task-relevant behavior, identifying functional mechanisms responsible for specific predictions. Frameworks such as Automated Circuit Discovery and Compression (ACDC) automate this search by isolating the smallest subgraphs that reproduce model behavior [32,63]. These methods provide causal evidence by showing that targeted manipulation of internal pathways leads to measurable behavioral changes, advancing mechanistic interpretability beyond correlation [40,44].

## 4.3 Representation Analysis
Representation analysis complements intervention-based methods by examining model activations to translate latent states into interpretable, human-aligned terms. A key approach is the logit lens (or direct logit attribution / unembedding lens) [39,65], which applies the model's unembedding matrix to intermediate hidden states to estimate token distributions at each layer. These intermediate predictions reveal how outputs evolve with depth-early layers show diffuse distributions, while deeper layers become more confident and accurate [41]. Such analyses help locate where specific semantic information emerges, showing that transformer models typically progress from feature extraction to integration and specialization across layers [37]. Since the standard logit lens assumes linear compatibility with the output layer, enhanced variants like the tuned lens improve interpretability by recalibrating projections for each layer [65].

## 4.3.1 Attribution Paths and Activation Lens.
Attribution-path and activation-lens analyses form the observational counterpart to the interventional techniques of Sections 4.2.1-4.2.2. These methods trace how activations evolve and which components contribute to them-answering Where did the evidence flow? rather than What happens if we change it?. The activation lens projects hidden states into human-aligned conceptual spaces such as embedding bases or learned semantic directions [29,66], enabling interpretation of what a given layer encodes. For example, cosine similarity between a residual vector and token embeddings can reveal whether the layer emphasizes objects, emotions, or spatial relations [36]. Attribution-path techniques-such as input-level methods like Integrated Gradients, attention flow, or logit attribution-quantify how inputs and intermediate layers influence outputs [22]. Integrated Gradients, while not mechanistic, attributes output variation to input features and thus serves as an observational tool that highlights which input patterns activate downstream circuits. These attribution methods complement mechanistic analyses by guiding where causal interventions should be tested. Aggregating these paths across layers reconstructs influence chains (e.g., an output derived 30% from layer 3 and 70% from layer 5). Although correlation-based, they remain invaluable for generating hypotheses and narrating functional explanations that later motivate interventional validation. Together, attribution and activation-lens analyses illuminate concept evolution through depth, complementing causal tools to provide a holistic understanding of deep model behavior.

## 4.3.2 Attribution Paths.
Attribution paths are mechanistic interpretability techniques that trace how input features or internal components contribute to activations or outputs. Unlike static feature importance measures, they analyze the model's computation graph to quantify causal influence across layers [22]. A key method, integrated gradients, computes the average output gradient as inputs interpolate from a baseline to their true values, producing smooth and theoretically grounded attributions. In transformer models, attribution paths extend through attention heads and residual streams to map token-to-token and layer-to-layer information flow [67]. Examples include attention flow and logit attribution, which aggregate multi-hop attention or decompose logits into additive contributions [68]. These methods enable fine-grained causal tracing, bridging observed model behavior with its underlying computational mechanisms.

## 4.4 Feature Decomposition Techniques
To address feature entanglement and polysemanticity described in Section 3, researchers employ SAEs and dictionary-learning approaches to decompose activations into interpretable, humanaligned bases [33,62]. These methods learn sparse latent features that correspond to distinct semantic components of a model's internal representations. SAEs reconstruct activations through a compressed sparse hidden layer, promoting monosemanticity by activating only a small subset of units. For instance, Cunningham [33] trained SAEs on language-model residual streams, uncovering neurons responsive to interpretable attributes such as quoted speech or topical categories. Dictionarylearning variants (e.g., sparse coding, Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF)) similarly extract basis vectors aligned with coherent semantic or visual properties like edges or colors [29]. Recent work extends these ideas to transformer attention components. Kissane [62] applied SAEs to GPT-2 attention outputs, revealing that single heads encode multiple functional roles that can be disentangled into distinct features (e.g., long-range context vs. local syntax). Intervening on SAEderived activations has also been shown to elicit targeted, causally valid behavioral changes [44]. Building on SAEs, Transcoders [69] and Crosscoders [70] extend feature decomposition across layers and model variants. Transcoders learn layer-wise sparse mappings that reveal how features transform through the network hierarchy, while Crosscoders align latent dictionaries between base and fine-tuned models to trace which features persist, split, or emerge after adaptation. These architectures capture cross-layer and cross-model feature relationships absent in conventional SAEs, providing a foundation for automated circuit discovery in large-scale transformers.

## 4.5 Toy Models and Synthetic Tasks
Toy models and synthetic tasks provide controlled environments where the computational mechanisms learned by neural networks can be fully reverse-engineered and validated. These small-scale testbeds, such as parity functions, formal languages, and group operations, offer known groundtruth circuits, enabling researchers to verify whether interpretability tools recover the correct internal logic [71]. For example, Chughtai [43] trained models on finite group composition and extracted the exact representation-theoretic algorithm learned by the network, while Friedman [72] demonstrated that Transformer Programs trained on sorting tasks could be translated into humanreadable Python code. Such settings have been instrumental in uncovering core mechanisms behind in-context learning. Olsson et al. [37] identified the emergence of induction heads in small Transformers trained on synthetic sequences, linking their appearance to phase transitions in training loss and validating their causal role via ablations. Singh [73] further dissected these circuits by perturbing intermediate activations, revealing the minimal structural components required for induction behavior to emerge. Toy models also facilitate the study of feature superposition and polysemanticity. Elhage et al. [36] used a sparse Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) network to show that once the number of features exceeds the available neurons, representations become entangled via linear superposition. ## 5.1 TransformerLens
TransformerLens is a popular open-source python tool, which aims to help mechanistic interpretability of transformer-based language models, especially those with GPT-style architecture [79]. It was designed by Neel Nanda, and the core feature of the library is the HookedTransformer class. Each transfer layer can be broken down into three types of blocks: Attention Block for multi-head attention mechanism, MLP Block for feed-forward network, and Residual Stream for upholding the current state which gets carried out in the next state. Here, every intermediate stage is assigned explicit hook names, which allows consistent fine-grained access in the system. Hook is a python program which can read, modify activations, and also can log or save activations for offline analysis. During a forward pass, these hooks give researchers a strong means of accessing, cacheing, and manipulating the internal activation of the model. This allows methods like activation patching, where activations from a different run replace parts of the model's activations, to test hypotheses about the function of specific components causally. The library is built on PyTorch, and it aims to be extensible and user-friendly while supporting a wide spectrum of open-source models. Its' main objectives are to enable scientists to understand the circuits that support the behavior of the learned algorithms and to reverse-engineer them. TransformerLens has evolved as a fundamental instrument in the field of mechanistic interpretability by offering tools for in-depth circuit tracing and intervention. The TransformerLens ecosystem has Neuroscope, CircuitsVis, and Systematic Analysis of Interpretable Latents (SAIL) to complete the work-setup cycle. This architecture of TransformerLens is lightweight, consistent in naming, and integrates well with customized training, synthetic data, and pre-trained model [80].

## 5.2 Neuroscope, CircuitsVis, and SAIL


## 5.2.1 Neuroscope.
Neuroscope is built as an open-source interactive visualization tool built for analyzing individual neurons and features inside transformer models. It is developed by Anthropic (Chris Olah and collaborators) as an integral part of their research work on interpretability of LLMs. Neuroscope offers a collection of the maximum activating dataset examples for every neuron in several LLMs [81]. It is primarily used for visualizing and browsing individual neurons and features. It also examines datasets that activate a neuron in better manner.Along with that, it also inspects how neurons align with human interpretability concept. In this purpose, it compares features on neuron across the model. Although Neuroscope is not an interactive intervention tool, it is an essential first step in many interpretability studies, offering the preliminary findings that can subsequently be examined using more interactive tools such as TransformerLens. While TransformerLens focuses on transformer's overall activation, neuroscope focuses only on the feature discovery and visualization [80].

## 5.2.2 CircuitsVis.
CircuitsVis is an open-source library for creating interactive visualizations for mechanistic interpretability research. It helps to understand the internal feature of transformer model by generating visuals of activation patching effects, attention pattern, logit lens output, attribution decomposition, and residual stream projections. In the core architecture of CircuitsVis, python library is used in the backend for generating data structure. In the frontend, web-based javascript library TypeScript, React, and D3.js are used for rendering and visualization. It is designed to work within python environments like Jupyter notebooks and allows for the visualization of attention patterns, tensor values, and other model internals [81]. Developed as a fork of the unmaintained PySvelte library from Anthropic, CircuitsVis provides a standardized way to create the kinds of visualizations that are common in interpretability papers, such as attention head patterns and circuit diagrams. The library is built with React, and its modular design allows researchers to create custom visualizations for their specific needs. This is light-weight to use, reproducible and it's full interactive visualizations have made it a user-friendly tool [82].

## 5.2.3 SAIL.
SAIL is a framework developed by Redwood Research, which is designed for scalable and modular analysis by integrating tools for activation extraction, patching, and visualization. It is used in mechanistic interpretability to have better understanding of the information processing of a large neural network. It provides a systematic way to identify the meaningful features and understand their impact on the model's behavior. It facilitates researchers to identify, cluster, trace interpretable features, such as neurons, attention heads, and residual stream components, within transformer-based language models. Researchers use it for analyzing induction heads in small GPT style transformer. The core part of SAIL is a SAE. This encoder is trained for compressed representation of high-dimensional activations so that each latent can capture distinct disentangled features. When the SAE is trained, each latent is examined for feature identification. Then, by applying activation pinching, it identifies the particular heads which are responsible for desired output [83]. Along with that, SAIL's visualization features help to identify the influencing features on output prediction by using logit attribution. At the core of SAIL lies a SAE, which plays a central role in revealing latent features from high-dimensional MLP activations. The encoder transforms residual stream activations into a sparse, overcomplete latent representation, while the decoder reconstructs the original signal from a learned dictionary of feature vectors. This disentangled representation promotes interpretability by isolating individual functional components within the network. As shown in Figure 7, this architecture enables researchers to decompose transformer computations into human-interpretable parts, supporting downstream tasks such as activation patching and causal attribution.

## 6 Applications
Mechanistic interpretation enables developers to identify hidden risks in models, thereby obtaining the exact solution and enhancing the alignment between AI and humans. This is a new concerning topic since it is related to the credibility of the model's output and also understanding between AI and humans, where trust, safety, and model editing play a crucial role. ## 6.1 Alignment and Safety
A simple question-is it correct to depend on one model for understanding mechanistic interpretability? In that case, Chughtai et al. [43] showed that depending on e, their mainline S5 model can provide information about two of the possible circuits used to solve the task out of a possible six. However, after studying more models, the insights into the mechanism of a single task were understandable. The ACDC algorithm, which identifies abstract units in circuits responsible for underlying behaviors, also aids in novel interpretability work, involving subgraphs for understanding and predicting neural network behavior through the underlying algorithm implemented by models [32]. Tracing attention heads in IOI is a multi-task approach where the initial stage starts with a haphazard algorithm and a base circuit. Then, prompt variants are used to challenge its assumptions and analyze head-level attention pattern where key discovery S2 Hacking was introduced. S2 Hacking reveals the IOI circuit's high performance on the variance, while IOI is expected to fail. These findings can help understand the full model's performance, which can increase safety [85]. For safety assessment, Neuron to Graph (N2G) can play a crucial role in the interpretability of machine learning models. N2G facilitates a graph that can capture a neuron's behavior, can mitigate the "illusion of interpretability", and evaluate the level of challenges and correctness [61]. The above cases validate how mechanistic interpretability plays a pivotal role in the application of safety and alignment for the model's output. Recent research further highlights the fragility of alignment mechanisms in large language models. Wei et al. [86] demonstrate that safety-critical parameters occupy only a sparse subset (about 3%) of model weights, and pruning or modifying these regions significantly compromises safety while largely preserving utility. Their findings emphasize that alignment brittleness can emerge from the structural separation of safety and utility regions, suggesting that future interpretability-driven safety frameworks should integrate robustness evaluation across such disentangled regions.

## 6.2 Debugging and Model Editing
Mechanistic interpretability enables targeted debugging and editing of neural networks by identifying and modifying internal circuits responsible for specific behaviors. A recurring phenomenon observed during training is grokking, where models transition from memorization to generalization after extended training despite initially poor generalization. This process-analyzed through metrics like restricted and excluded loss-reveals phase transitions between memorization, circuit formation, and cleanup, offering a window into how functional algorithms emerge in small transformers [46]. Understanding these transitions helps pinpoint whether a model is stagnating or progressing toward robust generalization. Gradient-based interpretability also supports debugging by revealing vulnerable or misfiring components. For example, [63] used adversarial samples to expose GPT-2's biases and overprediction tendencies in acronym-generation tasks, illustrating how mechanistic probes can trace fault sources. A complementary line of work, Rank-One Model Editing (ROME) [88], introduced a principled framework for locating and editing factual associations in transformer MLP layers. By applying minimal rank-one weight updates, ROME preserves model coherence while selectively correcting internal representations-a key advance for mechanistic model editing. Follow-up approaches extend this paradigm to multi-fact and low-rank updates, strengthening the connection between causal localization and safe model modification. Another emerging framework, DIFFLENS, identifies and surgically edits neurons responsible for biased activations using gradient-based interventions [87]. It modifies feature activations through scalar adjustments rather than retraining, enabling efficient correction without degrading global performance. Together, these techniques-from circuit-level debugging to localized weight editingillustrate how mechanistic interpretability bridges diagnosis and repair, offering fine-grained control over model behavior while maintaining interpretive transparency.

## 6.3 Scientific Discovery
The goal of mechanistic interpretability is to uncover a network's internal computations. When fully reverse-engineered, such networks are termed Mechanistic Neural Networks (MNNs). MNNs learn differential equations through a Mechanistic Block, bridging prediction and understanding, and linking machine learning with scientific reasoning for equation discovery [89]. To interpret LLM mechanisms, [69] introduced Transcoders, enabling circuit-level analysis through MLP sublayers. Ref. [46] revealed that models solve modular addition via Fourier space representations, using trigonometric rotation to perform addition. Systematic circuit analysis across models can yield periodic table features that generalize network behavior [43].

## 6.4 Interpretable Training Objectives
Attention Output SAEs are a potential tool for mechanistic interpretability to provide sparse and interpretable reconstructions of models where disentanglement is facilitated through training objects. SAEs are efficient with breaking down attention outputs into sparse representations and also reveal context of attention layers, such as long-range context or induction behaviors. By understanding polysemanticity (multiple functions per head), SAEs provide insights about the IOI circuit, and a new technique, Recursive Direct Feature Attribution (RDFA) for tracing computations. Their training objectives align with the intention of uncovering new areas for analyzing and interpreting transformer internals since their priority is in delivering sparsity and fidelity, ensuring interpretable and faithful reconstructions [62]. The IOI task, e.g., John gave a bottle of milk to Mary, serves as a canonical benchmark for probing syntactic reasoning and compositionality in LLMs like GPT-2 [62,64,74]. Through techniques such as activation patching [63], causal tracing [15], and tools like TransformerLens and ACDC [32], researchers have isolated the causal subcircuit underlying IOI behavior [74]. The IOI case highlights both methodological advances in mechanistic discovery and theoretical insights into circuit-based computation in transformers, reinforcing the need for fine-grained tools such as SAEs to resolve interpretability illusions and disentangle latent features [62,90].

## 7.3 Superposition in Toy Models
Neural networks can represent more features than available neurons by encoding them in overlapping directions within activation space, a phenomenon known as superposition [15,77]. The Toy Models of Superposition framework [77] provides a controlled setting to study this effect, showing how sparse inputs and ReLU activations enable efficient yet ambiguous feature compression. As illustrated in such models, increasing feature sparsity allows the network to represent more concepts within limited capacity, but also intensifies polysemanticity, where units become responsive to multiple features [44]. To mitigate this, SAEs and related dictionary-learning methods can re-express activation spaces into more interpretable bases [62,90]. These techniques recover disentangled features that approximate monosemantic representations and allow targeted causal testing. Applied to toy networks, they validate theoretical predictions about feature overlap and reveal how superposition arises naturally under representational constraints.

## 7.4 Vision and Multimodal Models
Although mechanistic interpretability has been most extensively explored in natural language processing, similar analytical frameworks have begun to illuminate the inner workings of computer vision and multimodal models. In the vision domain, Olah et al. [11] introduced the Circuits framework, decomposing convolutional neural networks into interpretable feature circuits composed of curve, texture, and object-part detectors. This line of work demonstrated that convolutional filters can be organized into hierarchical substructures with compositional semantics, offering a mechanistic understanding of representation building within deep vision models. Subsequent research extended these ideas to Vision Transformers (ViTs), where attention mechanisms replace convolutional inductive biases. Chefer et al. [91] proposed a gradient-based relevance propagation method that traces patch-level dependencies across transformer layers, revealing how attention heads aggregate local and global visual cues. Similarly, Raghu et al. [92] compared the representational geometries of ViTs and convolutional networks, showing convergent hierarchical organization despite architectural differences. Together, these studies illustrate that interpretability techniques such as relevance propagation, probing, and circuit analysis generalize beyond textual domains. Mechanistic inquiry has also expanded to multimodal architectures, where models jointly process visual and linguistic information. Goh et al. [66] discovered multimodal neurons in CLIP that respond to both text and image stimuli of the same concept (e.g., the word and image of Spider-Man), evidencing shared latent concepts across modalities. Complementary visualization frameworks such as VL-InterpreT [93] further reveal cross-modal attention flows, enabling causal inspection of how visual and textual tokens interact within transformer layers. Across these domains, the goal of mechanistic interpretability remains constant: to uncover causal, compositional, and semantically grounded structures within learned representations. Whether in attention circuits, convolutional feature maps, or multimodal neurons, these efforts collectively extend mechanistic analysis from language to vision and beyond. Case Study of Chinchilla-70B Circuit Analysis (Failure Case): Mechanistic interpretability has begun extending to frontier-scale models such as Chinchilla (70B), introduced by Hoffmann et al. [94] as a compute-optimal alternative to Gopher. Subsequent analyses sought to trace neuronand head-level circuits underlying Chinchilla's in-context learning behavior, but found that interpretability at this scale remains highly fragile. Replication efforts by Besiroglu et al. [95] revealed that circuit attributions were sensitive to data distribution and token-formatting changes, while only a minute fraction of the model's computation could be causally validated. Later work by Sharkey et al. [44] described this as a streetlight-effect limitation-analyses illuminate only the interpretable subset, leaving most computation opaque. These findings underscore that, despite the promise of large-model studies, mechanistic interpretability in frontier systems remains context-dependent, partial, and difficult to generalize, emphasizing the need for scalable and automated causal-mapping tools.

## 8 Challenges and Limitations


## 8.1 Scalability to Large Models like GPT-4
A fundamental challenge for mechanistic interpretability is scaling its methods to today's extremely large models. While early successes in reverse-engineering networks were often demonstrated on small architectures or toy tasks, it remains unclear how to feasibly apply these techniques to state-of-the-art models such as GPT-4, which carry orders of magnitude more parameters and complex emergent behaviors. The manual and semi-automated workflows that suffice for analyzing a 6-layer or 12-layer model become impractical for models with hundreds of layers or attention heads. In practice, mechanistic insights have largely been limited to smaller language models (e.g. GPT-2 or curated mid-sized models) [96]. Indeed, commentators have warned that progress in interpretability has not matched the rapid progress in AI capabilities, raising the risk that current methods will fail to generalize to cutting-edge systems [15]. As model size increases, so does the combinatorial complexity of their internal states,a single forward pass through GPT-4 yields billions of activations, making comprehensive inspection prohibitively costly with current tools [44]. The sheer size of LLMs introduces qualitative differences that complicate interpretability. Scaledependent capabilities rely on distributed patterns far more complex than the small circuits mapped in earlier work [97,98]. Techniques effective for million-parameter networks often break down once parameters reach GPT-4 scale. Without systematic automation, interpretability risks falling behind rapid capability gains.

## 8.2 Ambiguity due to Polysemantic Neurons
Mechanistic interpretability faces persistent ambiguity from polysemantic neurons-units that encode multiple, sometimes unrelated, concepts simultaneously [34,99]. A single vision neuron might respond to both dog faces and car wheels, while a language neuron could track distinct syntactic or semantic roles. This arises from superposition, where networks compress many features into shared, entangled directions within limited representational dimensions [44,77]. As a result, neuron activations seldom correspond to one interpretable property, complicating feature-level explanations. To mitigate such ambiguity, SAEs re-express activations as a larger set of monosemantic latent features [47,62]. Although SAEs improve interpretability, disentangling features in large networks remains difficult and assumption-dependent [100]. Recent work by Chanin et al. [101] identifies a related failure mode termed feature absorption, where hierarchically related latents in SAEs cause more specific features to absorb their parent concepts. This results in latents that appear interpretable yet fail to activate in expected contexts, reducing recall and interpretive robustness. Recognizing and mitigating such absorption effects is increasingly crucial for ensuring the stability and fidelity of mechanistic analyses in sparse and high-dimensional models.

## 8.3 Subjective Nature of Interpretability
Mechanistic interpretability often hinges on human judgment, as ground-truth explanations for a model's internal computations are rarely available. Researchers must rely on intuition and domain knowledge to hypothesize the function of neurons or circuits, making interpretability vulnerable to inconsistency and disagreement [102]. This subjectivity can lead to persuasive but potentially misleading narratives that do not reflect the model's true mechanisms [4]. Evaluation further suffers from the absence of standardized metrics. Criteria like simplicity, coherence with known features, or predictive usefulness are applied inconsistently across studies [103]. Manual tuning and interactive workflows introduce confirmation bias and hinder reproducibility, as small changes in analysis decisions can yield diverging conclusions [16]. The hand-crafted nature of many studies raises concerns about the reliability of their findings, particularly in the absence of falsifiable methods or observer-independent validation. This limitation underscores a central need for scalable, systematic, and rigorous interpretability pipelines [44].

## 8.4 Absence of Standardized Benchmarks
Unlike other machine learning domains that rely on canonical benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet, GLUE), mechanistic interpretability lacks standardized tasks and evaluation protocols. This gap stems from the absence of known ground truth internal mechanisms in most real-world models, making it difficult to assess whether an interpretability method is accurate or generalizable [103,104]. As a result, most studies rely on isolated case analyses, limiting reproducibility and hindering systematic progress [100]. Recent work has introduced synthetic benchmarks to address this gap. Tracr translates human-readable algorithms into transformer models with known internal circuits, enabling precise validation [105]. Similarly, RAVEL tests whether SAEs can disentangle polysemantic neurons into distinct latent features [47]. While these tools show promise, they are not yet widely adopted and may oversimplify the challenges posed by large, open-ended models [100]. The field has yet to converge on shared evaluation goals: i.e., prioritize recovering circuits, predict behavior under interventions, or match human intuitions. Inconsistent success criteria across papers restrict comparative insight [15]. Developing robust, widely accepted benchmarks remains a critical step toward improving reproducibility and scientific rigor in mechanistic interpretability. Mechanistic interpretability can be considered a specialized form of post hoc interpretability that focuses on uncovering the internal computations of trained models rather than solely their input-output correlations. While earlier sections (see Figure 2) clarify its white-box nature, this comparison highlights how mechanistic interpretability extends traditional post hoc analysis by linking observed behaviors to explicit causal structures within the model. Post hoc interpretability methods explain model predictions by analyzing input-output behavior. Popular techniques such as LIME and SHAP approximate feature importance using local decision boundaries [7,9], while Grad-CAM [106] and its variants [107, 108] extend these ideas to vision tasks by highlighting spatially influential regions. These methods have seen broad adoption in domains including healthcare [109,110], remote sensing [111], thermal comfort modeling [112], and cognitive disorder detection [113]. Their strength lies in model-agnostic flexibility, yet they can produce approximate or unstable explanations sensitive to sampling and perturbations [114][115][116]. In contrast, mechanistic interpretability directly investigates circuits, activations, and architectural pathways within models [14,117], using techniques such as activation patching, causal tracing, sparse feature discovery, and neuron ablation [13,104,118]. These methods reveal latent circuits for linguistic and safety-relevant behaviors [15,119], though their scalability remains limited despite tools like TransformerLens [14] and MechIR [118]. Overall, post hoc methods provide accessible yet approximate insights, whereas mechanistic interpretability offers faithful, causal understanding with greater manual effort and lower scalability [44,120].

## 9.2 Mechanistic vs. Explainable-by-Design Models
Explainable-by-design models embed transparency into their architecture, as seen in decision trees, linear regression, symbolic reasoning, and rule-based systems [121,122]. They provide direct insight into decision processes through interpretable parameters or logical rules, ensuring validation and regulatory compliance [123,124]. However, these models struggle with complex, high-dimensional tasks where deep learning excels. Mechanistic interpretability addresses this gap by decoding internal computations of high-performance black-box models without simplifying them. In architectures like transformers, it enables structure-aware understanding via feature attribution and circuit tracing [125,126]. This approach reconciles performance with transparency, offering post hoc interpretability when inherently explainable models are infeasible. Recent studies further integrate mechanistic methods with interpretable training objectives that promote modular and sparse representations, uniting explainable-by-design and mechanistic paradigms [40,104].

## 9.3 Mechanistic Interpretability vs. Probing and Representation Analysis
Representation analysis and probing form a major axis of interpretability research, aiming to uncover semantic information encoded in neural representations. Linear probes trained on hidden activations test whether linguistic or semantic properties are linearly decodable [121,127], though their presence does not guarantee causal use due to the selectivity-informativeness tradeoff [114,128]. Mechanistic interpretability addresses these limits by tracing how information flows and is used in computation, as shown by Wang et al. [13] in identifying attention heads and MLP components for coreference resolution in GPT-2, and by Parry et al. [118] in linking latent circuits to document relevance. Unlike static probing, mechanistic methods enable interventions such as ablation or activation patching, providing causal insight into how models compute. Together, probing guides exploration, while mechanistic interpretability delivers functional and causal understanding of model behavior. Mechanistic interpretability has made significant theoretical strides in elucidating the computational substrates of modern neural networks. Nonetheless, substantial open problems remain, particularly in terms of scalability, automation, and integration with adjacent disciplines. This section explores four major research trajectories that could shape the future of this field.

## 10.1 Automation in Circuit Discovery and Labeling
Automating circuit discovery and interpretation remains a key challenge. Existing methods demand manual effort, domain expertise, and heuristics. Although path and activation patching reveal causal structure, they lack scalability. Automated pipelines combining feature decomposition, causal tracing, and unsupervised clustering could generalize across models [44,60]. Models capable of autonomously labeling and validating discovered circuits would further ease interpretability bottlenecks in deployment and auditing.

## 10.2 Scaling Tools to LLMs and Vision Models
Another crucial direction involves scaling existing interpretability techniques beyond small toy models to LLMs and computer vision architectures. Most mechanistic insights thus far have been derived from relatively small-scale transformer models or convolutional neural networks. Applying these methods to frontier models like GPT-4 or multimodal architectures such as CLIP presents both technical and conceptual challenges. These include memory constraints, architectural heterogeneity, and the prevalence of high-dimensional superposition in learned representations [15,45]. Overcoming these limitations will require new tooling for sparse probing, scalable circuit tracing, and cross-modal alignment.

## 10.3 Extending Mechanistic Interpretability to Multimodal and Generative Models
While the field's recency partly explains the gap, fundamental technical and epistemic barriers hinder mechanistic interpretability in multimodal and generative architectures. Diffusion, autoregressive, and decoder-VAE pipelines rely on iterative or stochastic denoising loops, distributing responsibility across sampling steps [15], whereas conventional methods-activation patching, circuit tracing, or logit-lens inspection-assume a single deterministic pass [32]. Multimodal transformers entangle modalities through shared embeddings and cross-attention, blurring causal boundaries between visual and textual features [36,44]. Generative models encode content in high-dimensional latents mapped via nonlinear decoders, where interventions often dissipate or distort causal effects [64]. Moreover, their set-valued outputs weaken notions of necessity and sufficiency, while scaling exacerbates polysemanticity and computational demands [15,64]. Promising directions include time-indexed intervention toolkits for diffusion and decoders, cross-modal causalscrubbing tests, multimodal SAEs, trajectory-aware lenses, synthetic multimodal benchmarks, and standardized evaluation protocols for open-weight models.

## 10.4 Integration of Neuroscience and Cognitive Theory
Mechanistic interpretability shares methodological parallels with systems neuroscience, particularly in how both disciplines analyze distributed computation via causal intervention and response characterization. Future work may benefit from closer integration with neuroscience, including the use of analogues such as receptive fields, functional motifs, and network modularity [11,14]. Moreover, cognitive science can inform interpretability by providing models of symbolic reasoning, memory, attention, and language structure. This cross-disciplinary synthesis could lead to more structured hypotheses about circuit function, particularly in high-level reasoning and abstraction.

## 10.5 Development of Inherently Interpretable Models
Beyond post hoc interpretability, there is growing interest in designing models that are inherently interpretable by construction. These models prioritize modularity, disentanglement, and transparency in their internal representations. Techniques such as SAEs, slot attention, and information factorization encourage alignment between internal mechanisms and semantic concepts [14,36]. Additionally, architectural inductive biases can be incorporated to favor decomposable circuits, monotonic functions, or interpretable embeddings. Moving in this direction may enhance safety, debuggability, and regulatory compliance in high-stakes applications.

## 11 Conclusions
Mechanistic interpretability has evolved from isolated case studies on small networks to a rapidly maturing research programme that now probes billion-parameter models. This survey mapped the field along three abstraction layers-neurons, circuits, and full algorithms-and three empirical regimes-behavioral, counterfactual, and causal-providing a unifying vocabulary for future work. We catalogued the methodological toolbox (manual tracing, intervention-based analysis, sparse feature discovery, and toy-model testbeds) and reviewed the open-source frameworks that have enabled recent progress. Despite these advances, four bottlenecks persist: (i) the combinatorial scale of modern architectures, (ii) polysemantic representations that thwart neuron-level explanations, (iii) heavy dependence on human judgment, and (iv) the absence of community benchmarks for fidelity and robustness. Addressing these gaps will require (a) automation pipelines for scalable circuit discovery, (b) training-time incentives or architectural priors that favour monosemantic features, (c) rigorous, falsifiable evaluation suites grounded in causal tests, and (d) cross-disciplinary collaboration with neuroscience, programming-language theory, and systems engineering. Looking ahead, we foresee three promising research frontiers. First, interpretability-first pretraining: integrating sparsity, modularity, or causal regularisers directly into optimization objectives. Second, hybrid mechanistic-statistical auditors: combining the global coverage of attribution methods with the local precision of causal patching to certify safety-critical behaviors. Third, societal-scale benchmarks: evaluating how mechanistic insights translate into policy, governance, and human-AI alignment outcomes. Progress on these fronts will be essential for keeping interpretability on pace with the escalating capabilities of frontier models. If successful, mechanistic interpretability can move from a diagnostic afterthought to a core design principle, providing transparent, auditable, and ultimately more trustworthy AI systems.
